Privacy is becoming impossible to protect in the modern age of technology and surveillance.  
  
Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the statement and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true and explain how these considerations shape your position.

As of 2025, over half of the world's population owns and utilizes a smartphone. Devices like these are becoming one of our most intimate extensions of ourselves; offering a [[Connection]] to our loved one's no matter the distance, but this [[Connection]] to the outside world goes two ways. The prompt states that privacy is becoming impossible to protect in the modern age of technology and surveillance, and I mostly agree with the statement for the following two reasons. However, I concede that there are some methodologies (however extreme) to keep private in this digital age. 

Firstly, the ability to keep one's life private is becoming quite laborious due to the tools at the disposal of corporations and the government. For example, in the 2016 elections, [[Social Media]] profiles and activity (albeit non-private information) were utilized by Cambridge Analytica to build profiles on users in order to sway political voting. This exemplified that enough private information could be deduced from a third party without inside knowledge on individual users to make them change their opinions and beliefs. Similarly, the practice of targeted advertising by companies such as Google through their AdSense program follows the same trend of turning user interaction on any supported website into personal insight of preferences regardless of user consent. The desire to keep some information private whilst still being able to interact and connect with others via [[Social Media]] and the web is not uncommon, though as stated above, is quite difficult if not impossible to achieve.

Secondly, our locations are easier than ever to track; with no foolproof way to avoid this tracking our locations are thus impossible to keep private. One such vulnerability includes the very smartphones we depend on. Any device connected to the internet without a virtual private network (VPN) is vulnerable to location triangulation (a process in which cell tower [[Connection]] history is utilised to determine exact location to the meter). Even with a virtual private network, the internet service provider (ISP) has access to the location in which the final network ping originates from and can be requested via warrent in any lawful case. Similarly, due to the decrease in cost of camera technology, the quantity of surveillance cameras has burgeoned throughout any sophisticated area. The impact of this has only been escalated by computer vision and camera feed processing software which has enabled the tracking of subjects between camera feeds; all together weaving a network of interconnected tracking tools for the utilization of anyone with access to them. Our location is one of the most private pieces of information we hold dear, as it paints a direct picture into one's daily activities. 

All is not without [[Hope]] on the privacy front, however; tools like the aforementioned VPN and the Tor network allow for private web surfing and anti-surveilance devices such as the faraday bag (isolates devices such that no signals can escape) and computer vision breaker's (as demonstrated by YouTuber Benn Jordan) have been developed to counter this widespread surveilance. The problem is not just with the surveillance tools we know about, however, rather the tools we don't. 

As technology grows more and more sophisticated, it becomes exponentially harder to keep our lives private. Our internet activity provides a direct insight into our preferences and our devices and local areas are subject to everpresent surveilance. While some methods exist to counter these privacy violations, it has become quite extrenuous if not impossible to lead a fully private life short of isolation. Without policy changes to prevent this we risk falling into a surveilance state, though, as exemplified, those who value privacy will always fight back.
