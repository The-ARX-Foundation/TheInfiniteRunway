## If Kantian ethics emphasizes acting out of duty and adherence to universal moral laws, can genuine moral worth exist in actions motivated by emotions like love or empathy, even if they align with the moral law?
As the concept of duty is defined in Kantian ethics simply as the necessity of an action performed out of respect for the moral law, an action motivated **purely** by [[Love]] (or any other emotion or 'inclination') cannot be found to have moral worth. As finite rational beings (those being mortals influenced by finite rationality and emotion), however, I would argue that we cannot ensure emotion and duty are mutually exclusive without resorting to external tools (such as chance or these frameworks which we are currently studying). Utilizing the tools of Kantian ethics against that though; if one were to universalize a maxim of the utilization of chance or any sole framework for decision-making, the kind of world would we live in would be that devoid of autonomy, contradicting Kant’s emphasis on rational agency. By this logic, it can be extrapolated that emotion is not the cause of immorality / the lack of moral worth of an action, but the lack of the duty associated with it. Thus we circle back to the ideal scenario that is Kantian ethics, though identify it as paradoxical in natural reality.

Concluding, under Kantian ethics, actions motivated purely by [[Emotions]] cannot have moral worth. As finite rational beings, ensuring the separation of duty and emotion necessitates external tools. If universalized, the use of these tools would contradict the autonomy central to Kantian ethics, thus revealing a **paradox** within the framework. This challenges the practicality and coherence of Kant’s moral theory when applied to real human experiences.
