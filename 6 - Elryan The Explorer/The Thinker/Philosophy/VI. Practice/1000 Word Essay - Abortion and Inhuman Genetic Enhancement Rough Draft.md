**“Does the potential for personhood generate a duty to realize it?”**

### ✨ I. The Benatarian Void — Better Never to Have Been

Philosopher **David Benatar** argues in _Better Never to Have Been_ that coming into existence is always a harm. Why?

- Non-existence entails no suffering.
    
- Existence entails inevitable suffering.
    
- Thus, bringing someone into existence imposes harm they otherwise would not have endured.
    

From this vantage, **non-procreation is morally neutral—or even preferable**. No one is harmed by not being born. To give the serum, or to reproduce, is to impose life with all its burdens.

> **Application:** Withholding the cat-person serum isn’t morally wrong. You have not _harmed_ anyone. No being existed to be deprived.

### 🌱 II. The FLO (Future Like Ours) in Reverse — Are We Obligated to Create Flourishing?

Don Marquis’s FLO argument, when wielded in the positive, suggests that killing is wrong because it deprives someone of a valuable future.

But if we invert this logic:

- If not granting the serum = denying a potential FLO,

🛡️ **Counter:** No. We do not have an obligation to maximize the number of flourishing beings.  
To do so would obligate endless procreation, AI creation, or bioengineering—**an absurd conclusion**, as it ignores resource constraints, individual autonomy, and the intrinsic neutrality of non-being.

> This aligns with the **non-identity problem**: no one is wronged by not being brought into existence, because there is no “someone” yet to be wronged.

### 🧬 III. Kantian Dignity and the Act of Creation

Kant argues that **moral obligations are owed to rational beings**—those with ends of their own.

But here’s the twist: **Nonexistent beings have no ends**. No preferences. No claims.  
Thus, **creating a person is not a moral duty**, because no duty is owed to that which does not yet exist.

> Only _once_ personhood is actualized does Kant’s imperative apply.

You asked:

- Suffering ceases.
    
- There is no subject to mourn nonexistence.
    
- The universe loses no intrinsic value by the absence of humanity.
    

Thus: **There is no moral harm in the voluntary extinction of our species.**

This doesn’t mean it's desirable—but it does mean it’s not immoral.

## III. My Argument: A Threshold Model of Moral Standing (441 Words)
To resolve the moral tension posed by the FLO theory and Paske’s serum, we must distinguish clearly between **different stages of potentiality and actuality**. I propose a framework of **three ontological stages** relevant to moral standing:

2. **Initiated Potential** – A being exists in a transitional, developing state. Examples: a fetus, or a cat halfway through the serum’s transformation.
3. **Actualized Personhood** – The being has become a self-aware, temporally continuous, morally relevant subject. Examples: infants, adult humans, awakened cats.

My thesis is this: **Moral standing belongs only to stages 2 and 3.** Stage 1 does not warrant moral consideration, for one simple reason—**moral duties require a subject**. If no subject exists to whom a duty could be owed, there can be no moral failure in non-action.

This framework allows us to resolve the reproductive paradox posed earlier. Choosing not to reproduce—remaining at Stage 1—does not violate moral duties, because there is no being yet to be harmed. In contrast, once potential is **initiated** (Stage 2), we have entered morally significant territory, especially if that potential is clearly recognizable and tractable. This is where Paske’s cat becomes ethically interesting.

2. **Temporal continuity** – The being can anticipate and relate to its own future, grounding the very idea of a “future like ours.”
3. **Moral reciprocity** – The being possesses, or can plausibly develop, the capacity to engage in mutual moral relations.

This framework captures why we grant moral standing to infants (whose capacities are incipient but unfolding), while denying it to pigs or early fetuses (who lack the integrated trajectory toward personhood). It also explains why the **serum cat becomes morally important mid-transformation**—because it begins to exhibit, or visibly approach, these three faculties.

Thus, personhood is not a moment, but a **metaphysical threshold**—crossed when a being awakens into a **coherent, morally significant identity**. It is at this point, and not before, that we are bound by duties rooted in the FLO framework and [[Kantian Ethics]] alike.
