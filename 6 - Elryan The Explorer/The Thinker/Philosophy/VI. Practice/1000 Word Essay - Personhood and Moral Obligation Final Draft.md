## I. Introduction
If you could grant a cat personhood-level consciousness, would you be morally required to do so? Paske’s thought experiment challenges personhood-centered ethics—especially FLO theory, which ties moral status to the value of a potential future. If failing to bestow personhood deprives a being of a “future like ours,” has a wrong been committed?

I argue that **moral duties to potential persons arise only once certain thresholds are crossed**, and that mere potential is morally inert. To support this, I’ll examine FLO theory, Paske’s dilemma, and propose a threshold model that distinguishes between biological potential and morally significant personhood followed by my own personal objection and corresponding defense.

## II. The Problem of Potential: FLO and the Serum Dilemma
The **Future-Like-Ours (FLO) theory**, proposed by Don Marquis, claims that killing is wrong because it deprives the victim of a valuable future—one rich with experiences, goals, and meaning. This argument has been used in anti-abortion ethics, suggesting that even a fetus has a morally significant FLO.

But FLO faces ambiguity: who qualifies for such a future? Squidges? Animals? AI? The theory struggles to define which beings possess futures like ours, especially when applied to cases outside normative human development.

Paske’s personhood thought experiment makes this tension explicit. Suppose a serum could elevate a cat’s cognition to full personhood—granting it memory, autonomy, and a coherent self-narrative. Once transformed, the cat would clearly have a FLO. But if we choose **not** to administer the serum, are we guilty of depriving the cat of its future? If so, FLO implies not just a duty to refrain from harm, but a duty to **activate latent personhood**—a startling ethical claim.

Some extend this idea using **Kantian logic**: if persons are ends-in-themselves, and a being could become such a person through accessible means, then we may be morally obligated to realize that potential. The cat isn’t just a pet—it’s a dormant moral agent. One could even suggest that the **existence** of the serum generates a duty to use it.

This leads to uncomfortable territory: would choosing not to reproduce or enhance beings also be morally blameworthy? Does every potential person impose a duty?


## III. A Threshold Theory of Moral Standing
To resolve this dilemma, I propose a **threshold model** that distinguishes between types of potential and their moral implications. There are three ontological stages relevant here:

1. **Pre-Initiation** – No subject yet exists (e.g., unfertilized gametes, a cat without access to the serum).
2. **Initiated Potential** – A being is undergoing transformation or development (e.g., a squidge, or a cat mid-serum).
3. **Actualized Personhood** – A self-aware, temporally continuous moral subject emerges.

Only stages 2 and 3 hold moral weight. Stage 1 lacks a subject to whom duties could be owed. This distinction explains why abstaining from reproduction is morally neutral—there is no being to harm or help.

Yet initiated potential—**when a being is clearly and concretely on a path toward personhood**—does impose moral responsibilities. In the serum case, the cat becomes ethically significant not at the start, but when it begins showing signs of cognitive transformation.

To ground this, I propose a **triune threshold** for personhood:

1. **Self-awareness** – The ability to recognize oneself as a persisting subject.
2. **Temporal continuity** – Awareness of one’s future and life trajectory.
3. **Moral reciprocity** – Capacity for ethical engagement with others.

These faculties justify moral standing because they establish a being as a **participant in the moral community**, not merely a vessel of experience. Sentience alone is insufficient; pigs may be sentient, but they do not show the integrated moral arc that personhood requires.

This framework explains why infants, though underdeveloped, possess moral status: they are on a clear, uninterrupted path toward personhood. The same may apply to the cat—_once_ transformation begins. Before that, it is no more morally urgent than unused reproductive material.

Under this framework we have no duty to maximize personhood not merely when it is possible, rather just to **respect it once it emerges.**

## IV. Objection and Reply
**Objection – _“The mere existence of such a serum would generate an obligation to use it.”_** 
A critic might contend that once we acknowledge the transformative power of the serum—and its capacity to create personhood from a non-person—we are morally bound to use it. If the serum-cat can be brought into personhood, withholding the serum seems akin to denying a being its rightful FLO.  Should we not then treat the serum-cat, or even the pre-serum cat, as already morally significant? 

I initially felt drawn to this view of obligation, as if there is any inherent value to the lives we live and the state that is personhood; why wouldn't we want to spread it?

**Reply – Withholding Is Not Harming** **// Potentiality Does Not Necessarily Incite Duty** 
This view falters on its definition of moral obligation, however, by failing to distinguish between failing to benefit and causing harm. The serum-cat, prior to transformation, resides in Stage 1 or early Stage 2—potential, not person. Withholding the serum is morally akin to choosing not to reproduce: a neutral act. No subject yet exists who can be wronged, and thus no duty is owed.

Paske’s imperative also stumbles into absurdity if taken seriously. If mere potential demands actualization, then we must reproduce constantly, lest we "withhold" infinite possible FLOs; ethics may permit creation, not require it. Thus, the serum's mere existence does not impose a universal obligation to use it. Moral duty awakens **with** the subject—_not before it_. Just as we are not morally bound to conceive every potential child, we are not required to activate every potential mind.

We do not have an obligation to maximize the number of flourishing beings.  To do so would obligate endless procreation, AI creation, or bioengineering—**an absurd conclusion**, as it ignores resource constraints, individual autonomy, and the intrinsic neutrality of non-being.  **We are not morally required to create persons, only to treat those who cross the threshold of personhood with the dignity they are due.**
