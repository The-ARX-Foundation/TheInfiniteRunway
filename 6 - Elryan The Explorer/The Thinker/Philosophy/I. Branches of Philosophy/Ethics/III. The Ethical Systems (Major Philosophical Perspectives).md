Themes: [[Philosophy]]

Over the centuries, various ethical systems have emerged, each offering a different path to understanding morality:

1. **Virtue Ethics (Aristotle)**: Focuses on developing good character traits (virtues). According to Aristotle, the aim of life is to achieve **[[Eudaimonia]]** (flourishing or [[Happiness]]), which is accomplished by living a life in accordance with virtue. The focus is on being a good person rather than following strict rules or evaluating outcomes.
2. **Deontology (Kant)**: Grounded in duty and principles, deontology holds that actions are morally right if they follow certain rules or duties, regardless of the consequences. Kant's **categorical imperative** instructs us to act in ways that we would will to be universal laws.
3. **[[Utilitarianism]] (Bentham and Mill)**: A consequentialist theory that argues that the best action is the one that maximizes [[Happiness]] or pleasure and minimizes pain for the greatest number of people. The rightness of an action is judged purely by its outcomes.
4. **Social Contract Theory (Hobbes, Rousseau)**: This theory views morality as an agreement between individuals to form a society, where certain rights and duties are mutually agreed upon. Justice, under this view, arises from the collective agreement to form rules for the common good.
5. **Ethical Relativism**: This view argues that moral values are not absolute but rather depend on cultural, social, or individual circumstances. What is right in one culture may be wrong in another, challenging the idea of universal moral truths.
6. **Existential Ethics (Sartre, Nietzsche)**: This perspective holds that individuals are responsible for creating their own values and meaning in an inherently meaningless world. It emphasizes **authenticity**, personal freedom, and the weight of moral responsibility.

Each system provides tools for evaluating ethical dilemmas, from personal choices to societal justice.
