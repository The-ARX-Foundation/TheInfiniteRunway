The foundations of ethical thought rest on a few fundamental concepts:

1. **Moral Goodness**: What makes an action morally right or wrong? Theories of morality seek to define the standards of good and evil, often grounded in **virtue**, **duty**, or **consequences**.
2. **Virtue**: Tracing back to Aristotle, virtues are qualities of character that lead to a flourishing life, such as courage, temperance, justice, and wisdom. A virtuous person acts rightly not because of external rules but due to the excellence of their character.
3. **Duty**: Central to many ethical systems, such as **[[Kantian Ethics]]**, duty refers to the moral obligations we owe to others, regardless of personal desires or outcomes. It emphasizes acting out of a sense of moral responsibility.
4. **Consequences**: Ethical theories like **[[Utilitarianism]]** argue that the moral worth of an action is determined by its consequences. What matters is the maximization of good outcomes, often framed as [[Happiness]] or welfare.
5. **Moral Autonomy**: The ability to make free and rational choices. Ethics presupposes that individuals are capable of moral deliberation and responsible for their actions.

By understanding these foundational concepts, one can begin to navigate the diverse ethical systems that have been developed throughout history.
