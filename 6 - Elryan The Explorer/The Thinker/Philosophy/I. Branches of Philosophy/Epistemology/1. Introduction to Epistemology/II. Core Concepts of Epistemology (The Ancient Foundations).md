Themes: [[Philosophy]]

The study of knowledge revolves around a few core concepts that form the basis of epistemological inquiry:

1. **Knowledge as Justified True [[Belief]]**: This classical definition, originating with Plato, asserts that to _know_ something, three conditions must be met:
    
    - It must be **true**.
    - You must **believe** it.
    - Your [[Belief]] must be **justified** (based on good reasons or evidence).
2. **Skepticism**: A fundamental aspect of epistemology is the skeptical challenge, which questions whether we can know anything with certainty. Philosophers like **Pyrrho** and **Descartes** have explored the limits of human knowledge, asking whether our perceptions and beliefs can ever be fully trusted.
    
3. **Empiricism vs. Rationalism**: Two ancient schools of thought dominate the discussion of how knowledge is acquired:
    
    - **Empiricism** holds that knowledge comes from sensory experience (e.g., Aristotle, Locke).
    - **Rationalism** claims that reason and intellect are the primary sources of knowledge (e.g., Plato, Descartes).
4. **Truth**: Epistemology concerns itself deeply with what it means for a [[Belief]] or proposition to be true. Theories of truth include the **correspondence theory** (truth is what corresponds to reality), **coherence theory** (truth is what fits within a system of beliefs), and **pragmatic theory** (truth is what works in practice).
    
5. **Certainty and Fallibilism**: The quest for certain knowledge has led philosophers to ask whether true certainty is possible. **Fallibilism** asserts that all human knowledge is potentially subject to error, while others, like **Descartes**, argue for certain foundations, such as the famous dictum, _cogito, ergo sum_ (I think, therefore I am).
    

By grasping these foundational concepts, one begins to navigate the complex terrain of how we come to know and understand reality.
