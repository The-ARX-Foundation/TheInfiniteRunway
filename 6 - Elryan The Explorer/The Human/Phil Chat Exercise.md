## Part 1: Conversation Plan / Prep Work

### Quotation / Passage
- Author: David Miller
- Reading: Immigration: The Case for Limits
- Quotation:
    > “A wider freedom of movement can also be justified by thinking about the interests that it serves instrumentally: if I cannot move about over a fairly wide area, it may be impossible for me to find a job, to practice my religion, or to find a suitable marriage partner. Since these all qualify as vital interests, it is fairly clear that freedom of movement qualifies as a basic human right.”

### Explanation of the Quotation / Passage
- In Your Own Words:  
    Miller is arguing that the right to move freely isn’t just valuable on its own, rather that it supports access to other critical aspects of life. Without it, people might be unable to meet their most basic needs or pursue deeply meaningful goals like employment, faith, or relationships. That’s why he concedes that freedom of movement could be considered a basic human right, not just a political or legal one; I would argue that should is a better term than could, however.
- Context:  
    This quote comes from the conclusion of Miller’s broader argument that states can legitimately place limits on immigration, but those limits must be balanced against fundamental human rights. Here, he’s exploring whether the right to immigrate (or at least move within borders) counts as one of those fundamental rights.
- Key Terms & Concepts:
    - Instrumental Justification: Valuing something for the other benefits it enables.
    - Vital Interests: Needs that are deeply important for a person’s well-being or flourishing.
    - Basic Human Rights: Rights that are considered universal and necessary for a dignified life.

### Analysis / Reflection on the Quotation
- Why This Quote?  
    I chose it because it highlights my viewpoint on immigration and brings up a tension in Miller’s own work; like he’s known for defending immigration limits but I respect that he also acknowledges why freedom of movement could be acknowledged as a human right.
- Why It Matters:  
    It’s a real-world dilemma that is currently effecting my (albeit extended) family! We need to find a better way to protect national interests without violating individual rights.
- Personal Reaction:  
    I find this argument compelling as previously stated. It made me reflect on how many freedoms are interdependent.
- Agreement/Disagreement:  
    I agree that movement is often instrumentally necessary, but I’d argue it's sometimes intrinsically valuable too--people value the ability to explore, migrate, and connect, even without a clear “need.” We are not truly free without this ability.

### Discussion Questions
1. Do you think freedom of movement is a basic human right? Why or why not?
2. Can a right be considered “basic” if it’s justified by its usefulness to other goals?
3. How should we balance the right to movement with a state’s right to control its borders?
4. Is there a difference between internal movement (within a country) and international movement (across borders)?
5. What counts as a “vital interest,” and who gets to decide?
6. Could restricting movement ever be morally justified, even if it harms someone’s access to vital interests?

## Part 2: Conversation Summary
- Who: I spoke with my wife, Kenya. She comes from a family of undocumented immigrants, which made her perspective especially valuable and personal.
- Length: The conversation lasted a little over 30 minutes. We mostly focused on the first discussion question, though the rest surfaced naturally through her experiences.
- Structure: I introduced the quote and asked, “Do you think freedom of movement is a basic human right?” That question alone sparked a flood of personal stories and insights (most I had already heard about but needed reminding for this assignment), especially around her parents immigration, [[Fear]], and dignity.

### Key Points Discussed
Kenya is from a family of undocumented immigrants. This has made it difficult for her as the [[Fear]] of deportation is rife. She brought up the concept of the cartels and the view of America as the land of the free, the desire of her parents for their children to have a better life. We shared solace in the fact that they have never committed any crime yet still feel the [[Fear]] meant to be instilled as a punishment to those who have. They currently work in construction at rates below their life expenses and are in serious poverty still here in the US. They are too afraid to raise those rates for [[Fear]] of losing the only customers they have, additionally in the case that someone gets mad and calls ICE that's the end of their lives as they know it (regardless of that life being suffering from my point of view). This has caused marriage issues and they kindof project it on the kids but that's besides the point. Kenya brought up DACA and that they procrastinated applying for it too long; that their notary procrastinated it actually. This has effected her trying to sustain herself as well sadly.

While we didn’t formally move through all six questions, we naturally touched on the relevant themes of the other questions, it was quite emotional and I didn't feel like pressing further. 

### Textual Connections
Kenya’s lived experiences reinforce my agreement with Miller’s counter-argument that freedom of movement enables access to vital interests. Her life has been directly shaped by a lack of that freedom--not because she’s legally barred from moving within the country, but because her family lives under constant fear that moving too much or speaking up could bring ICE to their door. While Miller allows (if not outright advocates for) limits on immigration, Kenya’s story highlights the moral and psychological costs of those limits. Her family has lived here peacefully for decades, yet cannot claim even the most basic forms of protection or stability. This conversation directly supports Miller’s acknowledgment that movement is so crucial to human flourishing that it constitutes a right. I'm quite convinced that there is something else at play with all of this policy and speculation; we cannot be seeing the whole picture if these atrocities are occurring.
### Learning / Development
- What I Learned:  
    This wasn’t just a philosophical exercise. It was a look into how these arguments affect the people closest to me. It deepened my understanding of the stakes behind the principles we discuss outside of our little bubble that is theory. I saw how the instrumental value of movement can shape every aspect of a life; from jobs, to safety, to mental health.
- Perspective Shift:  
    I entered this assignment thinking about movement in terms of rights and state duties. I left it understanding movement as something more existential: about [[Hope]], dignity, and being seen as fully human.
- Lingering Questions:
    - How do we fix a system that punishes people for trying to survive?
    - Can rights be restored after so much harm has already been done?
    - If procrastination by a notary can seal someone’s fate, what does that say about justice?
