## Abstract

- **Nature-inspired materials**: Efforts to understand, synthesize, and imitate natural objects or phenomena.
- **Purpose**: Gain improved insights into nature through materials, processes, or designs.
- **Materials**: Tangible and easily usable without complex engineering, as opposed to processes or designs found in nature.
- **Example**: Aquaporins, which are used for water filtration.
- **Focus of the work**:
    - Define, clarify, and consolidate the current understanding of nature-inspired materials.
    - Review examples from the laboratory to industrial scale.
    - Highlight emerging opportunities in this field.
- **Nature-inspired materials**: Have specific functionalities that involve electrical, mechanical, biological, chemical, or sustainable gains, or a combination of these.

## Introduction

- **Nature as Inspiration**: Nature has inspired countless processes, algorithms, materials, and designs for human use.
- **Nature-Inspired Products**: Many nature-inspired products are currently available in niche markets (see Fig. 1).
- **Terminology**: Terms like bioinspiration, biomimicry, and nature mimicry are used interchangeably but have subtle differences:
    - **Nature Prefixes**: Refers to both living and non-living natural systems.
    - **Bio Prefixes**: Specifically related to living organisms (biology).
    - **Relationship**:
        - "Bioinspiration" and "biomimicry" fall under "nature inspiration" and "nature mimicry."
        - The reverse is not true.
- **ISO 18458:2015(E)**: An international standard that outlines the differences between bioinspiration, [[Biomimetics]], and biomimicry.
    - **Inspiration**: The initial observation of nature that sparks creativity to mimic it.
    - **Mimetic**: The application of technology to create materials that replicate nature's functionality.
    - **Mimicry**: The most advanced form, using engineering tools to develop sustainable materials similar to those found in nature.
- **Multifunctional Mimetic Materials**:
    - Growing interest in multifunctional designs, such as self-cleaning buildings inspired by crystal structures (FCC, BCC, HCP) for strength.
    - Buildings can also take inspiration from natural structures like termite mounds, or from natural processes like lotus self-cleaning or photosynthesis in trees.
    - A holistic approach is needed to integrate these ideas into complex, multifunctional designs, and this research is still in early stages.
- **Review Scope**: Discuss the latest trends and advances in nature-inspired materials, focusing on design, manufacturing, and sources of inspiration.


## Broad Classification of Nature Inspiration

### **Nature-Inspired Processes**
Artificial processes that emulate natural phenomena.
- **Example 1: Artificial Photosynthesis**:
	- Inspired by plant photosynthesis (energy stored in chemical bonds).
	- Used for solar energy harvesting or solar-to-fuel conversion.
	- Example: Flower-like copper phosphate nanosheets functionalized with TiO2 nanoparticles act as a solar light harvesting device, splitting water into O2 and H2 for clean energy (similar to plant photosynthesis).
	- Comparison of natural vs. artificial photosynthesis (see Fig. 3a, b).
- **Example 2: Self-Cleaning Surfaces**:
	- TiO2 photocatalysts are used on surfaces (e.g., solar panels, walls) to degrade organic dirt and help water rinse the surface through hydrophilic properties under UV light.
- **Example 3: Biomineralization in Ceramics**:
	- Ceramics in nature form at mild temperatures via biomineralization, whereas manmade ceramics require high temperatures (over 1400°C) that can degrade material properties.
	- Geologically inspired biomineralization could enable low-temperature compaction of ceramics (e.g., calcium carbonate) for denser, better-quality materials.
	- Four-stage biomineralization process: dissolution, diffusion, precipitation, and plastic deformation (see Fig. 3c, d, e).
- **Example 4: Water Purification**:
	- Inspired by plant roots, which selectively uptake water and nutrients from the soil, inspiring methods for targeted chemical removal from water.
- **Other Processes**:
	- Energy storage inspired by biochemical systems.
	- Protein production inspired by spider silk.
	- Self-degrading plastic inspired by natural decomposition.

### Nature-Inspired Designs
Nature's design has influenced the development of functional products and systems, especially in the context of surface and structural designs.

- **Surface Design**:
	- Involves modifying a surface to change its properties, such as wettability.
	- **Example 1: Hydrophobic and Hydrophilic Leaves**:
		- Plants like purple bauhinia (Phanera pupurea) and water cabbage (Pistia stratiotes) (hydrophobic) and rosy periwinkle (Cathranthus roseus) (hydrophilic) exhibit changes in wetting behavior when coated with a thin copper film.
		- Hydrophobic leaves become more absorbent, while hydrophilic leaves show low absorbance or high reflectance due to the lack of nanostructures on their surface.
	- **Nanostructured Surfaces for Solar Absorbers**:
		- Leaf surface nanostructures reduce reflectance and increase absorbance, inspiring nanostructured coatings for better broadband absorbance in solar energy applications.
	- **Example 2: Jay Feather Patterning**:
		- Jay feathers exhibit different colors at different angles, inspiring surface patterning in designs.
- **Structural Design**:
	- Involves designing structures that enhance properties such as toughness and strength.
	- **Example 1: Toughness vs. Strength**:
		- In metals and ceramics, improving toughness often reduces strength. However, structural designs inspired by nature can balance these properties.
	- **3D Printing of Crystal Structure Architectures**:
		- Imitating the crystal structures found in nature through 3D printing in meso-microscale building blocks can replicate damage-tolerant properties seen in natural structures (e.g., bones, shells).

### Nature-Inspired Materials

- The development of nature-inspired materials aims to harness specific functions found in nature to enhance human-made products, contributing to sustainability.
- **Categories of Gain from Nature-Inspired Materials**:
	- **Electrical**: Materials that provide electrical functionality (e.g., energy storage or conductivity).
	- **Biological**: Materials inspired by biological processes or systems (e.g., biomimetic tissues, bioactive materials).
	- **Chemical**: Materials that facilitate chemical processes (e.g., catalysts or chemical sensing).
	- **Mechanical**: Materials inspired by natural mechanisms to enhance mechanical properties (e.g., materials mimicking bone or muscle).
	- **Sustainable**: Materials that help achieve [[Environmental Sustainability]] (e.g., biodegradable, energy-efficient materials).
	- **Multiplicity of Gains**: Some materials may provide multiple types of benefits, combining several of the categories above.
- **Categorization of Gains**:
	- The categorization helps position new developments in the field of nature-inspired materials, as shown in **Fig. 6** (referenced in the original text) and **Table 1** (not detailed here). This helps understand and classify the contributions of nature-inspired innovations to various technological and sustainability goals.

#### **Electrical Gain**
This category explores materials and systems that convert chemical or biological processes into electrical energy or functionalities.
- **Examples**:
	
	- **Electric-Eel Mimicked Hydrogel**: A polyacrylamide hydrogel compartment inspired by electric eels, which converts chemical energy to electrical energy. This research can pave the way for **self-powered body implants**.
	- **Nanomotor Inspired by Biochemical Reactions**: A nanomotor made of hyperbranched polyamide/L-arginine (HLA) that mimics endogenous biochemical reactions in [[The Human]] body. This nanomotor, with no waste discharge, can be used in various **biological applications**.
	- **Charge Storage with Photoproteins**: Electrical charge storage is achieved using photoproteins from **Rhodobacter spheroids**, with potential applications in **self-charging biophotonic devices** that combine charge storage and light harvesting.
	- **Bioinspired Neural Ion Transmission**: Research by **Teng et al.** on mimicking the transmission of nervous signals to simulate a neural information system, as illustrated in **Fig. 7** (not provided here).

These advancements in electrical gain are creating novel pathways for self-powered devices and applications that bridge biology and technology.


#### **Biological Gain**:
Nature-inspired materials in this category aim to achieve **biological benefits** by mimicking natural processes or structures.

**Examples**:

- **Bionic 3D Printed Corals**: Inspired by coral structures, these bionic 3D-printed materials promote **microalgal growth** in a space-efficient manner while achieving **outstanding photosynthetic quantum efficiencies**. This research aids both **coral reef studies** and the design of **photobioreactors**.
- **Self-folding Alumina Compacts**: Mimicking plant seed dispersal mechanisms, alumina compacts with bilayer architectures were created, capable of **shape change** during sintering. This approach helps in controlling the material's transformation.
- **Biodegradable Self-healing Hydrogels**: Designed for **tissue repair**, these hydrogels are made from **chitosan–cellulose nanofiber (CS–CNF)** composites with tunable self-healing properties. This innovation could lead to **better injectability** and enhanced **tissue regeneration** potential.
- **Tough and Adhesive Hydrogels**: Inspired by **plant catechol chemistry**, these hydrogels were designed to be both tough and adhesive, as shown in **Fig. 8** (not provided here). This research could enhance the development of materials for biological applications.

These biological-inspired materials can lead to advancements in areas such as **tissue repair**, **renewable energy**, and **bio-manufacturing**, enhancing sustainability and improving biological systems.

#### Mechanical gain

The **"Mechanical Gain"** section explores how nature-inspired designs can enhance the mechanical properties of materials such as strength, toughness, hardness, and durability. Examples include:

- **Osteointegrative Porous Struts**: A novel **porous strut** made from **hollow cylindrical nanohydroxyapatite/polyamide** was developed to aid in **cervical reconstruction**. These struts accelerate **osteointegration** (bone attachment), promoting faster healing and better attachment to the bone.
    
- **Insect Footpad Adhesive Devices**: Inspired by the **adhesive capabilities of insect footpads**, particularly those of **Drosophila** (fruit flies), researchers created a **spatula-like adhesive device**. This device uses a nylon fiber frame and gel material at the tip to replicate the **capillary forces** generated by the liquid-secreting hairs of insect footpads, providing strong adhesive properties to stick to surfaces.
    
- **Bone-Inspired Fiber-Reinforced Composites**: A design inspired by the **osteonal structure of mammalian bones** resulted in **fiber-reinforced composites** that improved **fracture toughness**. The composites featured **unidirectional glass fibers (UDGF)** embedded in a **carbon fiber (CF) sleeve**, arranged in an orthogonal direction to the main bone structure, enhancing material performance in transverse directions.
    
- **Spider Silk and Sarracenia Trichome-Inspired Materials**: The mechanical properties of **spider silk** were used to design materials for **fog water harvesting** and **enhanced mechanical properties**. **Sarracenia trichome**, which exhibits a hierarchical microchannel structure, inspired a material with superior **fog water harvesting** capabilities. Similarly, **spiderweb-like** and **cactus-like fog collectors** were fabricated using **direct laser structuring** and **origami techniques**, demonstrating novel applications in **moisture collection**.
    
- **Neural Probes and Brain-Machine Interfaces**: **Neural probes** inspired by nature were developed to facilitate **brain–machine interfaces (BMIs)**, aiming for improved **electronic integration** with the nervous system for medical and tech applications.
    
- **Nacre-Inspired Materials**: **Nacre** (mother-of-pearl) [[Architecture]] was mimicked to create materials that combine **optical transparency** with **mechanical toughness**, offering potential applications in both **optics** and **structural materials**.
    
- **Enamel-Inspired Nanocomposites**: **Columnar nanocomposites**, inspired by **enamel**, were created using **zinc oxide nanowire carpets** and a **polymeric matrix** to achieve mechanical properties comparable to natural enamel. These composites have the potential to improve the durability of coatings and structural materials.
    
- **Helical Fiber Actuators**: **Hierarchically arranged helical fibers** were developed to act as **actuators** that sense and respond to environmental stimuli like **solvents or vapors**, mimicking natural **response mechanisms** to external factors.
    

These innovations highlight how biological systems provide effective templates for enhancing material properties across various mechanical domains.

#### Multiplicity of Gains

The **"Multiplicity of Gains"** section explores materials that combine multiple functional benefits, much like how complex biological systems utilize several interrelated functions to operate efficiently. Key examples include:

- **Bionic Artificial Nerves**: These **artificial nerves** aim to replicate [[The Human]] sensory system, particularly for **mechanosensitive signals**. Built with an **electrical double-layer structure**, they minimize noise and offer precise signal transmission. Ongoing advancements will enable these nerves to sense **temperature, humidity**, and **light**, contributing to the development of **neuroprosthetics** and enhancing the functionality of **humanoids** and **intelligent robots**. This could revolutionize areas such as human-machine interfacing and sensory technology.
    
- **Electrotendon**: Inspired by the **human tendon**, an **electrotendon** was developed by integrating **single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs)** for **mechanical toughness** and **PEDOT:PSS** (a conductive polymer) for **electrical enhancement**. This material can endure over **40,000 bending-stretching cycles** without losing its electrical conductivity, showing promise in applications requiring both flexibility and durability, such as in **robotic actuators** and **wearable technologies**.
    
- **Hierarchical Materials Inspired by Murray's Law**: **Murray’s law**, which governs the branching patterns of natural systems like **plant stems**, **leaf veins**, and **vascular systems**, was applied to design **multiscale materials**. These materials feature pores that decrease in size across different scales, ensuring efficient connectivity from macro to micro levels. This **hierarchical structure** is envisioned to enhance performance in applications like **photocatalysis**, **gas sensing**, and **Li-ion battery electrodes**, by allowing for optimal **fluid flow**, **energy storage**, and **sensing capabilities**.
    

These materials, combining multiple functionalities, represent the future of multi-purpose systems, blending mechanical, electrical, and sensory properties to create complex, adaptive technologies.

### Holistic Nature-Inspired Approach

The **"Holistic Nature-Inspired Approach"** section presents an advanced perspective on **biomimicry** and **nature-inspired design**. Historically, mimicking nature's material structures aimed to directly replicate their functionality. However, current advancements emphasize a more nuanced, **holistic approach**, focusing on understanding and adapting natural processes rather than mere imitation. This shift integrates **design**, **materials**, and **manufacturing** as the core pillars of **engineering** and **nature-inspired** innovation.

Key insights include:

1. **Nature-Triggered vs. Engineering-Triggered Protocols**:
    
    - **Nature-triggered protocols** are **slow, bottom-up processes** driven by **thermodynamic equilibrium**, where materials grow, self-assemble, and adapt without human intervention. This approach allows natural materials to form complex, **hierarchical structures** (e.g., fibrous, helical, cellular elements), which balance strength and toughness.
    - In contrast, **engineering-triggered protocols** rely on pre-designed synthetic materials that prioritize inherent properties like strength and toughness but lack the adaptive, **sustainable** qualities found in natural materials.
2. **Advantages of Nature’s Design**:
    
    - **Multi-functionality**: Nature's materials, such as **peacock feathers** or **biological tissues**, demonstrate incredible **lightness** and **strength**, often incorporating **self-healing** properties and adapting to environmental changes.
    - **Sustainability**: The **bottom-up approach** in nature, where materials grow naturally under ambient conditions, requires **low energy** for fabrication, making natural materials inherently **sustainable** compared to energy-intensive synthetic materials.
3. **From Mimicry to Adaptation**:
    
    - The goal is not blind imitation but **adaptation** of nature’s principles. The process requires a deep understanding of how nature achieves its functionality before transferring these principles to engineering, which is now becoming more of a **fundamental design approach**.
4. **Biomimetic Design Process**:
    
    - The **biomimicry design spiral** and related frameworks (e.g., **DTU biocard**, **Biomimicry 3.8 DesignLens**) offer structured methodologies for nature-inspired design. Key steps include:
        1. **Problem analysis**: Identifying the design challenge.
        2. **Abstract technical problem**: Defining the functional requirements.
        3. **Transposing to nature**: Investigating how nature solves similar problems.
        4. **Selecting and understanding natural models**: Identifying and adapting biological models to the design problem.
        5. **Transposing to technology**: Translating the natural solution into engineering terms for practical implementation.
5. **Problem-Driven vs. Solution-Driven Inspiration**:
    
    - **Problem-driven** approaches start by identifying a problem and then seeking natural solutions.
    - **Solution-driven** approaches are inspired by observing natural phenomena (e.g., droplets on leaves or chameleon skin color change) and then applying these observations to solve engineering problems.
6. **The Role of Databases**:
    
    - Platforms like **AskNature** and the **Bionics System Database** help engineers access vast repositories of natural phenomena categorized by **function**. These resources aid in identifying potential natural solutions for engineering challenges.
7. **Challenges in Manufacturing**:
    
    - While nature-inspired designs are promising, they face challenges in **manufacturing**, particularly in the realm of **biomineralization** and other slow processes that fail to meet the **mechanical properties** of natural materials. Advances in fabrication technologies may overcome some of these limitations, leading to **more flexible**, **sustainable**, and **agile manufacturing processes**.

In essence, a **holistic nature-inspired approach** calls for a **synthesis** of understanding nature’s processes with **engineering design**, focusing not just on copying nature’s structures but also on adapting its processes for **innovative, sustainable solutions** in engineering.

## Author’s views and further prospects

The author emphasizes several forward-looking directions for nature-inspired design and materials, while acknowledging the immense potential and significant challenges in this field. Their views align with [[The Vision]] of unlocking the full capabilities of nature's intricate design systems by advancing both materials and manufacturing technologies. Below is a summary of the author’s key points and further prospects:

### Key Concepts

1. **Miniaturization, Hybridization, and Hierarchy**: Nature's construction methods excel in miniaturization (many functions in small volumes), organic-inorganic hybridization (strength and flexibility), and hierarchy (nano to millimeter scale network construction). These features need to be replicated more efficiently and practically in engineered systems.
    
2. **Material and Design Limitations**: Despite progress, nature-inspired materials still lack the hierarchical network characteristic of biological systems. Their practical realization on a commercial scale remains a key hurdle. Scaling up production affordably is a critical challenge that necessitates advancements in manufacturing techniques.
    
3. **Manufacturing Techniques**: The author categorizes manufacturing techniques into:
    
    - **Subtractive Methods**: Removing material from a bulk mass.
    - **Additive Methods**: Adding material layer by layer, such as in 3D printing.
    - **No Material Change**: Techniques that do not add or remove material.
    
    **3D Printing** is particularly promising for nature-inspired materials, allowing for highly precise, flexible designs, including structures inspired by biological forms (e.g., biomimetic scaffolds, cellular structures). The advent of **multimaterial multinozzle 3D printing (MM3D)** could expand the range of printable materials and improve scalability.
    
4. **Challenges in Precision**: Challenges persist in designing nature-inspired materials, particularly around the feature size limitations of current machines. Most commercial 3D printing technologies can only handle structures in the microns, while some biological structures are far smaller. There are also computational and experimental constraints when simulating the behavior of materials and predicting their real-world performance.
    
5. **Sustainability and Ecological Impact**: Natural materials often feature self-decomposition capabilities that do not harm ecosystems, unlike synthetic materials, such as plastics, which contribute to environmental pollution. The push toward nature-inspired materials must align with sustainability goals, ensuring that the materials used are recyclable and environmentally friendly.
    

### Future Directions

The author outlines several important future directions for advancing nature-inspired design and materials:

1. **Precision-at-Scale Production**: Achieving large-scale manufacturing of nature-inspired materials with high precision, essential for commercial viability.
    
2. **Recyclability and Longevity**: Ensuring that nature-inspired materials can match the longevity and recyclability of their biological counterparts, addressing sustainability in a deeper way.
    
3. **Digitization and Predictive Modeling**: Fully digitizing the design and manufacturing processes for nature-inspired materials, utilizing predictive modeling and simulations to guide production.
    
4. **Applications for Sustainable Development**: Identifying new sectors and applications for nature-inspired materials, particularly those that can help reduce carbon footprints and contribute to sustainable development goals (SDGs).
    

### Conclusion

The author envisions a future where the application of nature-inspired materials, processes, and designs could revolutionize industries by blending the efficiency of nature with the precision and scalability of engineering. However, this vision is contingent on overcoming significant hurdles in manufacturing techniques, precision at scale, sustainability, and the broader applicability of these innovations across various sectors.
