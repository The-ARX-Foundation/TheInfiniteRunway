# Reflection on Dr. Vanessa Restrepo's Guest Lecture on Bioinspiration
Dr. Restrepo introduced herself as someone who earned their success through [[Passion]] and effort; her being a female, non-native academic, I have a lot of respect for her being super educated and passionate about her work despite all opposition as mentioned in her introduction. Her lab 'BIM-Lab' (Biomedical Materials Lab) focuses on what she described as 'the inverse banana curve' of engineering materials.  The first bioinspired topic she introduced was that of the hook and loop (branded and commonly mis-referred to as Velcro) and its roots in the Burdock Burr. The hook and loop structures were described as a 'mechanical adhesive', the hooks small enough to slip between and catch on randomized fibrous loops. She was tasked to make a computational model of these hooks and loops to determine and utilize the mechanical response within simulation. She introduced her use of the CAD software 'abicus' to do so; she randomized the loops and ran simulations to acquire a vast quantity of tests to be averaged to form a statistically relevant mechanical response curve. She mentioned her tests assumed a sample could replicate the whole structure (minimal loops within simulation but plenty in real life) and normalized by area for translation to physical testing.
Transitioning to a more material science focused topic, nacre was introduced; a shell that has microstructure resembling brick and mortar consisting of ~95% aragonite and ~5% lustrin (glue) respective to the aforementioned brick and mortar. The lustrin was mentioned to make repeatable stress peaks, dissipating energy but repeating the stated peaks once closed and re-stretched. As a result of the structures it was observed that nacre possesses a toughness ~3x than that of pure aragonite under similar applied loads. Given her background in mechanical and civil engineering, along with the experience in hook and loop analysis, she thought to replicate the structure of nacre with hook and loop padded carmine bricks; I swear I misheard her but I believe it was stated to have resulted in a composite 50x tougher than carmine bricks alone. The structure was very compliant, giving three main benefits: reparability, cushioning, and compliance. The compliance lent itself to the desired self healing ability--furthermore the hook and loop being a mechanical adhesive provided the ability to remove individual bricks, thus reparability. Additionally the nature of the fibrous hook and loop structures provided very good compression metrics, acting like a cushion. 
Upon further testing with an alternative test-bench--that being magnetic layers separated by the hook and loop--multi layer applications of the tech were observed to possess inherent de-acceleration capabilities in compression and tension (mainly tension). This led to the discovery of a potentially re-usable shock absorption system in which multiple layers of the hook and loop fixed by outer supports could allow for safe de-acceleration of workers in situations of falling; shock prevention to avoid spinal injury caused by jolting the workers harness upon application of full body weight at momentum. 
The conversation then shifted to another source of bioinspiration: Octopus skin. The texture of this animal's skin possessed the capability to control a variable friction. Dr. Restrepo's initial attempts consisted primarily of nitinol shape memory, but this evolved into ideation of smart hooks (for hook and loop applications) with shape memory polymers; ideally leading to 'silent velcro'. I actually had some inspiration for this during the writing of this lecture and sent an email attempting to connect Dr. Restrepo with Dr. Ware's team. I believe the concepts outlined in his paper '[Material assembly from collective action of shape-changing polymers](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-023-01761-4)' (in which the team was able to utilize a material distribution akin to a thermostat's bi-layer metal strip as described in his lecture) to create temperature regulated actuators for the purpose of swarm assembly when activated may be utilized for the inverse of the activation displayed in the paper in her search for a reusable straightening hook to be used as a release mechanism on hook and loop applications. Hopefully I could be of some assistance in that [[Connection]]; though may be misunderstanding the technology. Regardless, I thanked both of them for their time and presentations, as both were greatly intriguing and appreciated.
