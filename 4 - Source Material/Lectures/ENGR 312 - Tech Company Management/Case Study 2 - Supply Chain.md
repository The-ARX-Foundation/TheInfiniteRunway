## Who Really Suffers When the Supply Chain Is in Crisis?

### Part 1
This excerpt reviews Peter S. Goodman's book, _How the World Ran Out of Everything_, which examines the global supply chain disruptions experienced during 2021–2022. The book focuses on three main points:

1. **Fragility of the "Just in Time" System**: Global supply chains, designed for maximum efficiency through minimal inventory (the "Just in Time" model), were left vulnerable when the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted normal supply and demand patterns. This lean production system left little room for error, contributing to massive delays and shortages across various industries.
    
2. **Profiteering and Resource Misallocation**: Large shipping companies and other key supply chain actors prioritized profits over global need, directing their resources where they could maximize returns rather than where they were most needed. This behavior exacerbated the supply chain crisis, especially for essential goods.
    
3. **Inequality in the Supply Chain**: The pandemic further revealed systemic inequalities within the global supply chain, where corporate elites profited while workers suffered harsh conditions. Goodman argues that this exploitation had been ongoing, but it took a global crisis for it to become undeniable to many, especially in wealthier nations like the U.S.
    

Goodman investigates how specific U.S.-based companies—such as a toy company and an almond farm—were affected by these supply chain disruptions. He also points out that the media's focus on the inconvenience to American consumers masked the fact that such disruptions have long been a reality for poorer nations.

The review critiques Goodman’s narrow focus on U.S. corporations as the primary beneficiaries of globalization, highlighting that Chinese business elites also significantly profited from these global supply chains, particularly during China's export boom.

The overall message of the book is that the pandemic's disruption laid bare the inherent vulnerabilities and inequalities of the global supply chain, which had long operated on exploitative foundations.


### Part 2
The latter half of this article explores the uneven impact of supply chain disruptions during 2021 and 2022, drawing attention to how wealthier nations like the United States and many European countries weathered these crises far better than poorer regions, especially in South Asia.

### 1. **US Supply Chain Resilience**:

While the book in question, _How the World Ran Out of Everything_, may paint a dire picture of global shortages, the author argues that the true experience of wealthier nations was quite different. Despite the supply chain shocks brought by the COVID-19 pandemic, disruptions in these regions were often short-lived and limited to specific product categories. The article stresses that the actual shortages in these countries were minor inconveniences rather than full-scale crises. In fact, the world’s richest corners never truly ran out of essential goods—panic buying, for example, was largely unnecessary. The author contends that, given the severity of the pandemic’s disruptions, the global supply chain held up remarkably well in these countries, an outcome worth deeper analysis.

Tim Harford's perspective is invoked here, as he emphasizes that even in early 2020, experts like MIT’s Yossi Sheffi remained optimistic about the supply chain's resilience. Harford concludes that, while there may have been shortages of items like kettlebells, the essentials such as food remained largely unaffected, even in the worst-hit periods of the pandemic.

### 2. **Energy Crises in South Asia**:

The narrative then pivots to regions where supply chain failures had far more devastating consequences—South Asia, particularly during the energy crises of 2022. These nations experienced severe power outages, leading to hospitalizations and deaths as the blackouts compounded the already unbearable [[Heat]] stress from rising temperatures. The situation in these countries is starkly contrasted with the relative stability seen in wealthier nations. This energy crisis wasn’t just about poor infrastructure but also about being outbid on global energy markets.

As the EU scrambled to replace its lost gas supplies following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, energy traders redirected much-needed fuel from South Asia to European markets, simply because European countries had more purchasing power. The result was that regions like Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan found themselves facing acute energy shortages, leading to widespread blackouts that had severe public health consequences. This unequal distribution of energy resources showcases the global nature of supply chain disruptions, where the response of one region (such as Europe’s pivot away from Russian gas) directly affects the fates of others.

### 3. **Global Interconnections and Wealth Disparities**:

The article closes by pointing out that the impacts of supply chain disruptions were deeply interwoven across the globe. The challenges faced by wealthier nations, while significant to them, were minimal compared to the devastation experienced in less affluent countries. This imbalance demonstrates how richer nations, with more resources and purchasing power, are far better equipped to navigate crises than their poorer counterparts. The story is ultimately one of inequality, where wealthier parts of the world fared far better, largely maintaining access to critical supplies and energy, while poorer regions were left to struggle with far graver consequences.

### Conclusion:

The crux of the article lies in this stark disparity: while much of the wealthier world faced inconveniences during the pandemic, poorer nations experienced life-threatening shortages and disruptions. A book titled _Why the World Did Not Run Out of Everything_ might better encapsulate the experiences of the West, where economic resources and institutional capacity cushioned the blow. Yet for countries like Bangladesh and India, these same disruptions were devastating, illustrating the deep inequality inherent in global supply chains. The West, the author concludes, remains in a position of relative advantage, even in times of global crises.


## Why Global Supply Chains Remain Vulnerable
In this revealing conversation with the Institute for New Economic Thinking, journalist Peter Goodman discusses his new book, _How the World Ran Out of Everything_, which addresses the persistent and critical vulnerabilities in global supply chains. Despite the world emerging from the worst of the COVID-19 pandemic, Goodman warns that we are still at risk of similar disruptions.

#### Key Vulnerabilities

Goodman’s book traces the evolution of global supply chains from the end of World War II, pointing out major shifts like the rise of shipping containers and trade agreements, such as China's entry into the WTO in 2001. These developments, while greatly enhancing global trade efficiency, have led to critical supply chain vulnerabilities. One key issue is the rise of "Just-in-Time" manufacturing, a principle that prioritizes efficiency by minimizing inventory. Although it was initially a successful strategy for companies like Toyota, Goodman argues that its widespread adoption—especially when driven by shareholder profit maximization—has dangerously weakened the resilience of supply chains.

The focus on cutting costs and slashing inventory has left supply chains fragile and highly susceptible to disruptions. Goodman explains how this approach contributed to severe shortages during the pandemic, from PPE for healthcare workers to essential medicines. The problem is not limited to a single sector; it spans industries as diverse as electronics, pharmaceuticals, and food production.

#### The Human Cost

The relentless pursuit of efficiency has also led to the dehumanization of labor, according to Goodman. McKinsey’s emphasis on “lean” principles, which prioritize reducing waste, often includes cutting wages and minimizing job security. Workers, particularly in warehouses and manufacturing sectors, are treated as expendable assets, subject to unpredictable schedules and reduced benefits, all to increase shareholder profits.

Goodman provides examples of this, such as the impact of "precision-scheduled railroading," which led to the firing of workers, reduction of rail services, and logistical inefficiencies that ultimately [[Hurt]] businesses. In one anecdote, rail cars were sent to the wrong locations simply to improve performance metrics, causing major delays for factories waiting on essential materials.

#### The Price of Shareholder Primacy

Goodman is particularly critical of how corporate practices, driven by shareholder-led profit maximization, exacerbate these vulnerabilities. In one striking example, he discusses a Minnesota factory that, at McKinsey’s advice, slashed inventory of $5 metal brackets used in hospital backup generators. When demand surged, the factory couldn't meet production targets, leading to costly expedited deliveries. The focus on immediate financial gain compromised long-term functionality and safety, particularly in critical sectors like healthcare.

#### Fixing the Problem

To address these vulnerabilities, Goodman calls for a fundamental rethinking of how supply chains are structured. Rather than focusing purely on cost-cutting and shareholder returns, he advocates for building resilient supply chains that prioritize human welfare and [[Environmental Sustainability]]. This would require overhauling corporate incentives to value long-term stability over short-term profits.

Goodman also stresses the need for government intervention, especially in times of crisis. During the pandemic, the government had to step in with industrial policies to ensure the production of vaccines. However, broader systemic reforms are needed to prevent future shortages and to distribute goods based on need rather than ability to pay.

#### Conclusion

The global supply chain remains vulnerable due to decades of prioritizing efficiency over resilience. The challenges exposed by the pandemic are far from over, and Goodman’s work underscores the need for significant reforms. Only by rethinking corporate incentives and re-establishing a balance between efficiency and stability can we [[Hope]] to prevent future supply chain crises.
