The Suprima chassis project was a substantial initiative meant to be a testament to Toyota's engineering and production prowess in North America. Toyota selected ChassisCo as a key supplier for critical components, but ChassisCo’s limitations in resource allocation and supplier management soon became apparent, jeopardizing the timeline and quality benchmarks that Toyota required.

#### 2. **Key Problems Encountered**

**Supplier Integration Failures**: Toyota's "go and see" principle emphasizes firsthand observation and engagement with suppliers to prevent surprises. ChassisCo, however, failed to maintain effective oversight, especially with second-tier suppliers, until problems had compounded and cascaded. Bernstein's late-stage second-tier visits reveal the consequence of this oversight failure: critical delays and quality risks.

**Production Readiness Issues at Tulsa Plant**: ChassisCo’s Tulsa plant was unprepared to handle the complexity and scale required by the Suprima project, lacking [[The Human]] and technical resources to meet production demands. This deficiency not only delayed production but also contributed to a deterioration in [[Trust]] between Toyota and ChassisCo.

**Chronic Quality Issues at Athens Plant**: The Athens plant’s quality lapses were severe. Despite automation and rigorous TPS testing, recurring quality issues—especially around the rear suspension cradle—surfaced in production and post-launch. This included defective welding, lack of standardized work, and weak inspection and feedback systems, all compounded by a lack of defect flowout control. These systemic flaws directly challenged the TPS values Toyota held.

#### 3. **Impact on Toyota's Operations and Brand**

The repercussions were tangible, especially with Toyota’s “bad news first” [[Philosophy]], which is central to their process improvement culture. The high defect rate (increasing from 565 to 2,845 per week) was a direct affront to Toyota's reputation for reliability and quality, demanding a rapid and effective solution to maintain brand integrity. The resource and cost strains faced by the Athens plant exacerbated tensions, with both Toyota and ChassisCo feeling the pressure.

#### 4. **Evaluating TPS Principles and Potential Limitations**

**Limitations of TPS in Supplier-Driven Issues**: The TPS model is robust for in-house quality improvement but was stretched by ChassisCo’s unresolved supply chain and internal operational challenges. ChassisCo’s structural disorganization and lack of a quality information system fell outside TPS’s scope and showed that a more integrative, interventionist approach might be required to restore performance.

**Potential TPS Adaptations**: Toyota’s role might expand to active supplier development, prioritizing "Jidoka" (automation with a human touch) to resolve ChassisCo’s issues with equipment and workforce allocation. Additionally, "Kaizen" (continuous improvement) could be encouraged within ChassisCo by deploying Toyota’s problem-solving teams, though this is resource-intensive.

#### 5. **Recommended Solutions and Strategic Adjustments**

- **Implement an Intensive Supplier Development Program**: Deploying Toyota-trained consultants to work alongside ChassisCo personnel, especially in critical problem areas like welding and defect flow control. This intervention would ensure TPS principles are integrated effectively.
- **Strengthen Communication and Feedback Mechanisms**: Toyota could enforce a structured defect feedback system and require weekly reporting on quality metrics, empowering both Toyota and ChassisCo to track real-time data on defect sources and containment.
- **Establish an Independent Quality Inspection Team**: A dedicated team on-site at Athens could monitor and address emerging issues promptly. This team would also oversee quality and process adherence within ChassisCo's second-tier suppliers.
- **Long-Term Investment in Workforce Training and Resource Allocation**: As ChassisCo struggled with sufficient human resources, training and development to bolster their workforce is essential for maintaining TPS levels of quality assurance, particularly if new models or processes are introduced.

In conclusion, this case underscores that even the most robust systems, like TPS, must be flexible and responsive to varying external challenges, especially when key suppliers are involved. Toyota’s intervention at ChassisCo would ideally blend TPS principles with targeted supplier improvement initiatives, ensuring ChassisCo's capabilities align with Toyota’s standards for quality, efficiency, and operational resilience.
