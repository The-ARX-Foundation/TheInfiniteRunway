### Accounting Methods
Inventory on balance sheet can be used to determine the health of a company.
SEC requires to document how this is documented
Methods:
	FIFO (First IN First Out) Costing
		Typically utilized for expensive / fluctuating materials
		- **Concept**: FIFO assumes that the first (or oldest) items purchased or produced are the first to be sold or used.
		- **First Cost Utilized**: The cost of the oldest inventory is assigned to the cost of goods sold (COGS). The remaining inventory is valued based on the most recent purchases.
		    - **Example**: If you bought 100 units at $10 each and later bought another 100 units at $12 each, and you sold 100 units, FIFO would assign the $10 cost to the COGS. The $12 units would remain in inventory.
		- **Advantages**:
		    - **Higher Net Income in Inflationary Periods**: Since the older, cheaper goods are sold first, COGS is lower, and profits appear higher.
		    - **Reflects Physical Flow**: This method often mirrors the actual flow of goods, especially for perishable items (e.g., food or pharmaceuticals).
		- **Disadvantages**:
		    - **Higher Taxes**: Higher profits in inflationary times mean higher taxable income.
		    - **Overstates Inventory Costs**: Remaining inventory can be valued at more recent (and often higher) prices, which might not reflect current market value.
	**LIFO (Last In, First Out) Costing**
		- **Concept**: LIFO assumes that the most recently acquired or produced items are sold or used first.
		- **Last Cost Utilized**: The cost of the most recent inventory is assigned to the COGS, while older inventory remains in stock.
		    - **Example**: Using the same scenario, if you sold 100 units under LIFO, the $12 cost (the most recent purchase) would be assigned to the COGS, leaving the older $10 units in inventory.
		- **Advantages**:
		    - **Tax Benefits in Inflation**: When prices are rising, LIFO results in higher COGS because the latest (more expensive) goods are sold first, reducing taxable income.
		    - **Matches Current Costs to Revenues**: Since the most recent costs are used for COGS, profits better reflect current economic conditions.
		- **Disadvantages**:
		    - **Lower Reported Profits**: Since COGS is higher, net income is reduced in periods of inflation.
		    - **Inventory Understated**: Older inventory costs (often lower) remain on the books, which may not accurately reflect current replacement costs or market value.
		    - **Restricted Use**: LIFO is prohibited under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), though it’s allowed under U.S. GAAP.
	**Weighted-Average Costing**
	Typically utilized for less expensive / not as fluctuating materials
	- **Concept**: Weighted-average costing smooths out price fluctuations by averaging the cost of all items in inventory.
	- **Self-Explanatory**: The cost of goods sold and the remaining inventory are based on the average cost of all purchases, not just the most recent or oldest ones.
	    - **Formula**: Average Cost per Unit=Total Cost of InventoryTotal Units Available for Sale\text{Average Cost per Unit} = \frac{\text{Total Cost of Inventory}}{\text{Total Units Available for Sale}}Average Cost per Unit=Total Units Available for SaleTotal Cost of Inventory​
	    - **Example**: If you bought 100 units at $10 each and another 100 at $12 each, the weighted average cost would be $11 per unit. When you sell 100 units, the COGS is $11 per unit, and the remaining inventory is valued at the same $11 per unit.
	- **Advantages**:
	    - **Smooths Price Fluctuations**: It provides a stable cost value, avoiding the sharp variances seen with FIFO or LIFO during periods of price volatility.
	    - **Simple to Apply**: It requires fewer calculations compared to LIFO or FIFO.
	- **Disadvantages**:
	    - **Less Accurate for Rapidly Changing Prices**: In times of significant price changes, it doesn’t reflect the true cost of inventory as accurately as FIFO or LIFO.
	    - **Can Dilute Cost Data**: In periods of inflation or deflation, this method averages out price differences, which may mislead financial analysis or profitability evaluations.

### Key Benefits of Automation, Robotics, Data Analytics, and AI in Supply Chain

1. **Enhanced Efficiency & Speed**:
    
    - **Automation** of repetitive tasks (e.g., sorting, picking, packing) speeds up processes, reducing human error and boosting throughput. Automated warehouses, like those at Amazon, demonstrate how robotics can fulfill thousands of orders per day with incredible accuracy.
    - **Robots** in warehouses can work continuously without fatigue, enabling 24/7 operations, greatly increasing productivity.
2. **Real-Time Data & Predictive Analytics**:
    
    - **Data analytics** enable real-time tracking of inventory, giving companies a clear view of stock levels, delivery times, and demand fluctuations. Predictive analytics help forecast future demand, enabling just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing and reducing excess inventory.
    - **AI** can analyze vast amounts of data, optimizing routes, predicting maintenance needs for equipment, and offering insights into supply chain bottlenecks, leading to better decision-making and reduced operational costs.
3. **Cost Reduction**:
    
    - Automation reduces labor costs, and AI-driven optimization minimizes waste (in time, resources, and materials). Robots lower human involvement in hazardous or repetitive tasks, improving safety and reducing the need for human oversight.
    - **AI-powered demand forecasting** reduces the cost of overstocking and understocking, ensuring optimal inventory levels are maintained.
4. **Increased Flexibility & Scalability**:
    
    - **Robotic systems** are modular, meaning they can be scaled up or down based on the demand. This flexibility allows supply chains to respond dynamically to fluctuations in market conditions, which is particularly beneficial for industries facing seasonal demand spikes.
    - AI algorithms can adapt to changing consumer behavior, optimizing inventory replenishment and logistics routes in real-time.
5. **Sustainability**:
    
    - Optimized processes through AI and data analytics can reduce carbon footprints by optimizing transportation routes, consolidating shipments, and decreasing energy usage within warehouses.
    - Robotics and automation can minimize material waste by reducing defects and ensuring consistent quality.

### Creative Ideas to Further Increase Supply Chain Efficiencies

1. **Autonomous Delivery Fleets**:
    
    - **Self-driving vehicles and drones** could revolutionize last-mile delivery. Imagine fleets of autonomous trucks transporting goods across long distances, coordinating with drones that handle the final delivery leg. This could drastically reduce delivery times and costs, especially in remote areas.
2. **Blockchain for Transparent Inventory Management**:
    
    - **Blockchain technology** could enhance transparency and [[Trust]] by providing an immutable ledger of every transaction and inventory movement. This would be particularly valuable for industries like pharmaceuticals or food, where traceability is crucial for ensuring quality and compliance with regulations.
    - Smart [[Contracts]] on blockchain could automate payments upon the delivery of goods, reducing the administrative overhead of invoicing and payment reconciliation.
3. **AI-Driven Dynamic Pricing & Inventory**:
    
    - AI could be used not only for predicting demand but also for **dynamic pricing** based on real-time market conditions. For example, if inventory is nearing surplus levels, AI could trigger discounts automatically to move excess stock before it becomes obsolete.
    - Additionally, AI could dynamically adjust manufacturing schedules and inventory purchasing, using real-time data to ensure optimal stock levels while reducing carrying costs.
4. **Augmented Reality (AR) in Warehousing**:
    
    - Workers could use **AR headsets** to visualize warehouse layouts, guiding them to the exact location of items, reducing the time spent searching for products. In combination with robots, AR could further optimize human-robot collaboration by directing workers to only handle specialized tasks while the robots manage repetitive duties.
5. **AI-Powered Sustainability Metrics**:
    
    - AI could track and optimize sustainability efforts by monitoring carbon emissions, energy consumption, and waste generation at every point in the supply chain. Companies could adjust logistics, sourcing, or production techniques to meet sustainability goals in real-time, creating more eco-friendly supply chains.
6. **Bio-Sensors and Real-Time Quality Monitoring**:
    
    - Integrating **biosensors** in the transport and storage of sensitive goods (e.g., food, pharmaceuticals) could provide real-time monitoring of temperature, humidity, and contamination risks. This would prevent spoilage or damage, ensuring the highest quality upon delivery.
7. **Predictive Maintenance with IoT**:
    
    - **Internet of Things (IoT)** sensors installed on critical supply chain equipment (e.g., forklifts, conveyor belts, drones) could detect early signs of wear and tear.     - Instead of centralized manufacturing, consider a network of **distributed micro-manufacturing hubs**. These small, highly automated factories could produce products closer to demand centers, using additive manufacturing (3D printing) and reducing the need for long-distance transportation. AI would coordinate production across these hubs, ensuring efficient use of resources.
9. **AI-Optimized Circular Supply Chains**:
    
    - **Closed-loop supply chains**, where goods are recovered, refurbished, and resold, could be managed through AI to maximize the lifespan of materials. AI could predict when products are likely to be returned or need refurbishment and adjust production and logistics accordingly, promoting sustainability and reducing waste.
