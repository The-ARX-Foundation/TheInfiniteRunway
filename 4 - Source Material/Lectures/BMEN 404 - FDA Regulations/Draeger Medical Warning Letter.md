WARNING LETTER 
CMS # 596529 
January 17, 2020 
Dear Mr. Dräger: 


- **Inspection Overview**
    - _Text_: “The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) conducted an inspection of your firm’s medical device operations, Draeger Medical Systems, Inc., located at 6 Tech Drive, Andover, MA, from August 20 through September 20, 2019.”
    - _Analysis_: This introductory sentence sets the context for the letter. FDA inspections are periodic and can be triggered by routine schedules, adverse event reports, or prior compliance issues. The FDA inspected Draeger’s Andover, MA facility over a month-long period, suggesting a detailed and thorough examination. Such extended inspections often indicate the FDA's focus on specific compliance areas or follow-ups on past observations.
- **Identification of Draeger as a Medical Device Manufacturer**
    - _Text_: “During the inspection, an FDA investigator determined that your firm is a medical device manufacturer of multi-parameter patient monitoring systems, including the IACS M540 ([[Infinity]] Acute Care System) and the ICS M300 ([[Infinity]] Central Station Wide).”
    - _Analysis_: By identifying Draeger as a “medical device manufacturer,” the FDA places the company under specific regulatory standards established by the FDCA and 21 CFR Part 820. This classification mandates compliance with the Quality System Regulations (QSR) — a framework of requirements that governs design, manufacturing, labeling, packaging, and post-market surveillance of medical devices. The FDA specifically mentions the IACS M540 and ICS M300, which are complex, multi-parameter patient monitoring systems intended to measure critical patient vitals, placing them firmly in the “high-risk” category in terms of regulatory scrutiny. This classification implies that any device-related adverse events or non-compliance issues could directly impact patient safety.
- **Legal Definition of a Device per Section 201(h)**
    - _Text_: “Under section 201(h) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the Act), 21 U.S.C. § 321(h), these products are devices because they are intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body.”
    - _Analysis_: Section 201(h) of the FDCA is central to the regulatory oversight of products that interact with or monitor [[The Human]] body. The FDA applies this section to ensure that products with diagnostic or therapeutic functions undergo rigorous safety evaluations before market release. The IACS M540 and ICS M300 meet this definition because they assist in diagnosing and managing patient health by monitoring physiological parameters. This classification triggers mandatory compliance with premarket submissions (like 510(k) or PMA), quality controls, and reporting of adverse events under FDA regulations. Given the high-risk nature of monitoring devices, especially in acute care settings, any malfunctions or errors could have severe health implications, which justifies the FDA’s focus on compliance for these devices.
- **Adulteration under Section 501(f)(1)(B)**
    
    - _Text_: “Our inspection revealed that the IACS M540 ([[Infinity]] Acute Care System) device is adulterated under section 501(f)(1)(B) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 351(f)(1)(B), because your firm does not have an approved application for premarket approval (PMA) in effect pursuant to section 515(a) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 360e(a), or an approved application for an investigational device exemption under section 520(g) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 360j(g).”
    - _Analysis_: The FDA classifies the device as “adulterated,” a serious violation that implies the device is being marketed without FDA-approved safeguards in place. Section 501(f)(1)(B) outlines that any Class III device lacking a PMA or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) is considered adulterated. Class III devices, like the IACS M540, usually support or sustain human life and thus pose a high risk if they fail or are improperly manufactured. This means that, legally, the IACS M540 cannot be marketed without FDA evaluation to confirm it is safe and effective for its intended use, particularly in critical care settings. The absence of a PMA or IDE approval indicates Draeger has bypassed necessary regulatory steps, which poses significant patient safety risks.
- **Misbranding under Section 502(o)**
    
    - _Text_: “The device is also misbranded under section 502(o) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 352(o), because your firm did not notify the agency of its intent to introduce the device into commercial distribution, as required by section 510(k) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 360(k).”
    - _Analysis_: The term "misbranding" suggests the device entered the market without FDA notification of intent via the 510(k) pathway, which is a critical step in confirming regulatory compliance for marketing in the U.S. Section 502(o) thus declares the IACS M540 misbranded, as Draeger failed to submit a 510(k) premarket notification. The 510(k) submission process is typically mandatory for devices that make modifications to existing products or newly introduce devices into commercial distribution. In this case, Draeger did not notify the FDA of their intent to distribute the device commercially, a major lapse that can lead to serious enforcement actions, such as recalls or import bans.
- **PMA Requirements and 510(k) Notification [[Satisfaction]]**
    
    - _Text_: “For a device requiring premarket approval, the notification required by section 510(k) is deemed satisfied when a PMA is pending before the agency. [21 CFR 807.81(b)]”
    - _Analysis_: Under 21 CFR 807.81(b), the FDA clarifies that if a PMA is submitted and pending, the 510(k) requirement is automatically met. However, the FDA’s statement implies Draeger did not submit a PMA. As a result, Draeger’s failure to submit either a PMA or a 510(k) represents dual non-compliance. Notably, the FDA explicitly references this regulatory clause to clarify that Draeger’s lack of both PMA and 510(k) filings exacerbates the regulatory breach.
- **Guidance and FDA Resource for Compliance**
    
    - _Text_: “The kind of information that your firm needs to submit in order to obtain approval or clearance for the device is described on the Internet at…”
    - _Analysis_: By pointing Draeger to a specific online FDA resource, the agency provides guidance on the regulatory steps required for compliant marketing of a medical device. This webpage likely details the PMA, IDE, and 510(k) processes, helping the manufacturer navigate what documentation and testing data are required to legally market their device. The FDA’s intent here is to direct Draeger to a pathway for achieving compliance, though the resource referral also reinforces the notion that Draeger’s existing submissions were incomplete or absent.
- **Timeline of Modifications and “Letters to File”**
    
    - _Text_: “Specifically, Letters to File dated May 1, 2013, January 15, 2014, April 27, 2015, and January 25, 2016, (b)(4).”
    - _Analysis_: Draeger’s internal documentation, marked as “Letters to File,” records modifications on multiple dates over a span of years. A “Letter to File” is often used by manufacturers to document changes to a device that they deem minor, typically not requiring an updated 510(k) submission. However, the repeated changes noted over these years hint at potentially substantial alterations. The dates also indicate that Draeger may have established a pattern of updating the device without regulatory notification, potentially underestimating the cumulative effect on device functionality and safety.
- **Impact of Device Modifications on Patient Safety**
    
    - _Text_: “The changes (b)(4), which may affect monitoring and result in patient injury or [[Death]]. For example, a patient with a life-threatening arrhythmia may not be detected or alerted adequately, resulting in adverse events.”
    - _Analysis_: The FDA flags the modifications as potentially endangering patient safety, specifically in high-stakes clinical scenarios like monitoring for life-threatening arrhythmias. This is critical for a multi-parameter patient monitoring system that healthcare providers rely on for timely alerts about acute changes in a patient’s health. If modifications compromised the system's ability to detect or communicate arrhythmias effectively, it could lead to undetected critical events, increasing the risk of morbidity or mortality. The FDA’s language here indicates that they consider the device changes significant enough to alter its risk profile, warranting closer regulatory oversight.
- **Requirement for 510(k) Notification due to Significant Modifications**
    
    - _Text_: “These changes represent significant modifications that could significantly affect the safety or effectiveness of the device such that a new 510(k) premarket notification must be submitted.”
    - _Analysis_: When a modification materially affects the device’s intended use or performance, the FDA mandates a new 510(k) submission, rather than reliance on a “Letter to File.” Draeger’s changes likely fit this criterion, as the modifications might influence the device’s core safety and effectiveness, particularly given its role in monitoring life-critical patient data. The FDA’s specific call-out here implies that Draeger’s self-assessment, documented only through internal letters, was insufficient, highlighting the need for regulatory review of these modifications.
- **Inadequate Response and Delayed 510(k) Submission**
    
    - _Text_: “In your firm’s response, dated October 10, 2019 and November 8, 2019, your firm notes that it will address all changes in one or more 510(k) submissions in accordance to discussions with FDA. Your response is not adequate because we have not yet received your 510(k) submission.”
    - _Analysis_: Draeger’s response to the FDA indicated a commitment to address all modifications in new 510(k) submissions, which suggests acknowledgment of the need for regulatory review. However, as of the date of this communication, Draeger had not submitted the 510(k), resulting in continued non-compliance. This delay could indicate procedural backlogs or an underestimation of the regulatory urgency on Draeger’s part, but it also signals to the FDA that Draeger’s response lacks the timeliness expected for high-risk device modifications.
- **Allegation of Adulteration Due to QSR Non-Conformity**
    
    - _Text_: “This inspection also revealed that the above devices are adulterated within the meaning of section 501(h) of the Act, 21 U.S.C. § 351(h), in that the methods used in, or the facilities or controls used for, their manufacture, packing, storage, or installation are not in conformity with the requirements of the Quality System regulation found at Title 21, Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), Part 820.”
    - _Analysis_: Section 501(h) defines a medical device as “adulterated” if it does not comply with the Quality System Regulation (21 CFR Part 820), which governs various aspects of the device lifecycle, from design to production and post-market surveillance. The FDA’s use of “adulterated” here implies a failure in critical areas such as manufacturing processes, [[Quality Control]], or facility standards. These deficiencies compromise the device’s quality and reliability, undermining its safety and effectiveness for clinical use. Notably, compliance with Part 820 is essential to ensure consistency and control over device specifications, thereby protecting end users—primarily patients and healthcare providers—from defective or substandard devices.
- **Identification of Responses and Key Stakeholders**
    
    - _Text_: “We received your responses dated October 10, 2019, November 8, 2019, and December 13, 2019, from Carsten Kossler, Vice President System Solution Research and Development, and Lida Reed, Director Quality, which responded to the Form FDA 483, List of Inspectional Observations issued to your firm on September 20, 2019.”
    - _Analysis_: Draeger’s internal response to the FDA 483 observations came from senior leadership—Vice President of System Solution Research and Development, Carsten Kossler, and Director of Quality, Lida Reed. This structured review timeline suggests that the FDA may have found the earlier responses lacking or incomplete, necessitating further clarification or corrective actions. - **Failure in Design Validation and Risk Analysis Protocols**
    
    - _Text_: “Failure to establish and maintain procedures for validating the device design that includes risk analysis, where appropriate, as required by 21 C.F.R. § 820.30(g).”
    - _Analysis_: Section 820.30(g) specifies that medical device manufacturers must validate the design of their devices, which includes conducting risk analyses to identify potential hazards, assess their impact, and implement mitigation strategies. Design validation ensures that the final product consistently meets user needs, intended uses, and safety standards. Draeger’s failure to maintain such procedures suggests a fundamental gap in their product development and risk assessment practices, which are essential for preventing post-market issues that could harm patients or compromise device performance.

For the FDA, a comprehensive CAPA should contain not only corrective measures but also a complete analysis of root causes, systemic corrections, and measurable effectiveness checks. This suggests that Draeger’s CAPA may lack a detailed assessment of the design process flaws that led to these issues, as well as broader preventive measures that would prevent recurrence. The FDA’s insistence on a more complete CAPA underscores the need for Draeger to develop a far-reaching, sustainable solution rather than isolated fixes.
