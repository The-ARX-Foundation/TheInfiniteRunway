**Below are the 6 case study questions in Dr. Patrick's lecture. Each question is worth 16.7 points. Please answer each of the questions/prompts with a few short sentences. Your goal is to demonstrate the new information/knowledge you have gained from the lecture in your responses.**

1. You have decided to submit your first abstract ever for a cardiovascular conference that you very much want to attend. Your research consists of measuring the P-selectin-mediated rolling of leukocytes on monolayers of endothelial cells. Endothelial cells are harvest from umbilical veins and leukocytes are obtained from patient and lab mate blood samples. Your data and analyses appeared great as the time drew near for you to write the abstract. You were excited and eager to proceed but your lab mates slowed you down, however, with a suggestion that you include a few more data points in the study. You agreed but voiced a concern that the submission deadline was coming up very soon. “Maybe you can use your own blood samples,” they said. “I mean, it’s just an abstract that you’re submitting, and you can obtain more blood samples between now and the conference and make changes if needed.”

**But would recruiting yourself be considered a conflict of interest or** **be somehow unethical?**

I see the appeal of using your own blood samples, but it could (most likely would) be perceived as a conflict of interest, as it introduces potential bias as self-sourced data may unconsciously lead to selective interpretations or raise ethical concerns about informed consent (you as both subject and researcher could be subconsciously downplaying risks or overlooking alternative data sources due to time pressures or personal investment). Including yourself could compromise objectivity, and reviewers may question the data, even if only for an abstract. It would be wiser to keep looking for third-party samples or seek IRB approval if self-recruitment is the only timely option.

2. In a weekly lab meeting a research assistant presents her experimental results to the group. You, the supervisor, notice that on one plot of a standard curve two data points have standard error bars whereas three do not. You ask if the standard deviation was so small that they are not observed on the plot (which can occur with graphics programs). The research assistant states that there are no error bars, that she only measured two experimental conditions. You asked where the other three data points came from. She admits, “I made them up because I knew what the curve is supposed to look like.” Everyone in the lab meeting goes silent and looks at you.

**What will you do? Has the research assistant done anything** **unethical?**

The research assistant’s creation of fictitious data points without empirical evidence is a severe breach of research ethics; it undermines the trustworthiness of the entire dataset! Fabricating results misleads interpretations and could compromise future research building on this work. The response should be to address the issue privately, emphasizing the importance of scientific honesty, and take some form of corrective action (i.e. requiring her to redo the experiment).

3. You and your co-workers are attending a social function composed of researchers that study the development of therapeutics for the rare cancer Merkel cell carcinoma. Naturally, general discussion begins about what the competing laboratories are working on. One of your co-workers brings up the latest therapeutic your group has formulated. The group is quite interested and your co-worker pulls out his phone and shows plots of the recent data acquired in animal trials as well as the drug’s formulation.

**What is your next action? Has your coworker done anything wrong?**

Assuming these individuals are not permitted to know this information otherwise, the co-worker's actions are ethically questionable as they breach confidentiality, possibly violating non-disclosure agreements and jeopardizing intellectual property rights. Disclosing unpublished data publicly risks leaking valuable competitive information and could hinder patenting or publication. My immediate action would be to pull the co-worker aside and remind them of these ethical boundaries (sharing of such information should only occur in vetted professional contexts with appropriate disclosures and permissions in place).

4. You are the principal investigator of a research team. You have collected data for a novel new imaging modality and are writing a paper to submit to Nature Photonics (a high-ranking journal). You are planning to be the senior and corresponding author and will have two other research staff who worked on the project listed as co-authors. Your immediate supervisor is excited about your work and requests that he be added as a senior author on the paper.

**What will you do? Is his request reasonable or ethical? Will your answers change if you are in line for promotion?**

The supervisor’s request may stem from interest or a desire for credit, but authorship should reflect actual contributions to research, not hierarchy. Authorship inflation dilutes the work of genuine contributors; if the supervisor contributed minimally, their addition could misrepresent the authorship credit and mislead readers about who truly led the study. I would respectfully discuss this concern and propose other acknowledgments if necessary. 
In the scenario that I'm in line for a promotion the facts dont change that it shouldn't be done. Yes it would be hard to do so, knowing I would likely be shooting myself in the foot, but my integrity and the integrity of my work should supersede all else.

5. You are participating on an NIH study section. Upon reviewing your cohort of assigned grants, you read of a clever experimental procedure being proposed that would solve a technical problem in your research that has been a stumbling block for 6 months. You consider sharing the procedure with your research staff.

**What will you do? Do you borrow the idea from the grant? Is it ethical to do so?**

The ethical codes of grant review prohibit using unpublished ideas presented in proposals, as it constitutes a breach of confidentiality and intellectual property theft. Sharing this procedure with my team, even informally, would be unethical and could lead to severe consequences if discovered, therefore I should address the technical problem independently or seek other published solutions. I would say though that especially if it would not impact the other team to just reach out and ask! People are usually pretty receptive to that kindof thing (given proper citations are done); offer to help out with peer reviewing the techniques and y'all could have a partnership in the works! The worst they can say is no then you're back where you started.

6. You are a research assistant in a cutting-edge private laboratory. The principal investigator of the laboratory has decided to do a study using human embryonic stem cells. She assigns you to the new project. Since this is a private laboratory there are no federal restrictions on using the cells, but your personal beliefs are in conflict with the use of embryonic stem cells.

**What will you do? What are your options?**

In this situation, I’d need to think about how my personal beliefs align with my work. If I’m uncomfortable with using embryonic stem cells, my first step would be to have an open conversation with the principal investigator. I’d want to explore whether there’s a way to adjust my role or, if possible, to be reassigned to a different project. If that’s not feasible, I’d consider stepping away from this particular study. Ethical integrity demands that we honor both professional and personal values; no research should compel us to compromise our deeply held beliefs.
